# Sound and Air Pressure

[Back to Physics](../README.md)

## Overview

Sound in air is a **longitudinal pressure wave**. Small pressure and density variations travel through the air while the associated particle motion is mainly back and forth around local positions.

The wave transfers energy without requiring the same air molecules to travel all the way from the speaker to your ear. Ordinary molecular motion and air currents still exist.

## 1. Atmospheric Pressure

Air consists mainly of nitrogen and oxygen. Molecular collisions produce pressure even when there is no audible sound.

**One standard atmosphere is 101,325 Pa**, but actual atmospheric pressure varies with altitude and weather. Sound pressure is a changing pressure component superimposed on this background.

## 2. Compressions and Rarefactions

| Phase | Diaphragm Motion | Effect on Nearby Air |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Compression | Outward | Pressure and density increase locally. |
| Rarefaction | Inward | Pressure and density decrease locally. |

A split second later, the membrane pulls backward, producing a region of lower pressure. This is not an empty vacuum gap.

```text
Diaphragm motion → compressions and rarefactions → traveling sound wave
```

Sound travels at approximately **343 m/s in air at 20 °C**. Its speed depends on the medium and conditions, including temperature.

## 3. How the Ear Detects Sound

1. **Outer ear and ear canal:** Guide sound toward the eardrum.
2. **Eardrum:** Vibrates in response to pressure differences.
3. **Middle ear:** The malleus, incus, and stapes transmit vibration to the inner ear.
4. **Cochlea:** Fluid motion produces waves that stimulate sensory hair cells.
5. **Auditory nerve and brain:** Carry and interpret the resulting signals.

A complex sound contains multiple frequencies; the hearing system does not simply track one universal frequency. See [NIDCD: How Do We Hear?](https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/health/how-do-we-hear).

## 4. Amplitude and Frequency

| Property | Physical Meaning | Perceptual Connection |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pressure amplitude | Size of pressure variations | Greater amplitude generally increases loudness. |
| Frequency | Number of cycles per second | Higher frequency generally corresponds to higher pitch for a pure tone. |
| Wavelength | Distance covered by one cycle | Depends on frequency and sound speed. |

A 60 Hz tone has 60 cycles per second; a 10 kHz tone has 10,000. Loudness also depends on frequency, duration, and the listener.

## 5. Why Sound Needs a Medium

Sound cannot propagate through a perfect vacuum because there is no material to carry the disturbance. Space is not uniformly empty: sound-like pressure disturbances can exist in gases or plasmas where conditions permit them.
