# Speakers and Electromagnetism

[Back to Physics](../README.md)

## Overview

A conventional **moving-coil speaker** converts an electrical signal into motion, then into sound. This explanation applies to dynamic drivers; other speaker and headphone designs use different mechanisms.

## 1. The Main Parts

| Part | Role |
| --- | --- |
| Permanent magnet | Produces a magnetic field in the gap where the voice coil sits. |
| Voice coil | Carries the signal current and experiences a force in that magnetic field. |
| Cone or diaphragm | Moves with the coil and pushes against the surrounding air. |
| Suspension | Supports and centers the moving assembly while allowing it to move back and forth. |

The diaphragm can be made from paper, plastic, metal, or other materials. In many speakers, a flexible surround and a component called the spider support it.

## 2. How Electromagnetism Creates Motion

Copper is not strongly attracted to a stationary fridge magnet in the way iron is. However, current flowing through a wire creates a magnetic field around it.

In a moving-coil speaker, the useful explanation is the **force on a current-carrying wire in an external magnetic field**. It is more precise than imagining two bar magnets facing each other and alternately attracting and repelling.

For a simplified speaker model:

```text
Force = magnetic flux density × effective wire length × current
F = B × L × I
```

Reversing the current reverses the force. The physical arrangement of the magnet and coil determines which direction corresponds to outward motion. See [Duke University's loudspeaker model](https://mems-teach-labs.pages.oit.duke.edu/controls-labs/site/labs/loudspeaker/loudspeaker/).

## 3. From an Electrical Signal to Sound

A DAC converts digital audio into an analog signal. An amplifier supplies the power needed to drive the speaker.

### Current in One Direction

1. Current flows through the voice coil.
2. The magnetic field produces a force on the coil.
3. The coil moves the attached diaphragm.
4. Outward diaphragm motion compresses the air in front of it.

### Current in the Opposite Direction

1. The current reverses.
2. The magnetic force reverses.
3. The diaphragm is driven in the opposite direction.
4. Inward motion produces a region of lower pressure in front of it.

These pressure variations propagate through the air as sound waves. Conventional current and electron motion have opposite directions in a metal wire.

### What Happens at Zero Voltage?

Zero voltage at one instant does **not** mean that the cone immediately stops or returns to the center. Coil inductance, the motion of the assembly, and the voltage generated by that motion affect its response.

Even at zero current, the cone can still be moving. The suspension provides a restoring force, while damping helps the motion settle after the signal ends.

## 4. Why Speakers Use Different Driver Sizes

Low-frequency sound at a useful listening level requires sufficient air displacement. A larger diaphragm can move more air for the same excursion. High-frequency drivers benefit from a small, light moving assembly.

| Driver | Typical Role | Design Considerations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Subwoofer or woofer | Bass and lower frequencies | Larger diaphragm area and sufficient travel to move air. |
| Midrange driver | Middle frequencies | A balance between displacement and high-frequency response. |
| Tweeter | Treble and higher frequencies | A small, light diaphragm designed for rapid vibration. |

Frequency ranges vary by design. A two-way or three-way speaker uses a **crossover** to divide the signal between drivers. Size alone does not determine bass performance: the enclosure, suspension, motor, and available power also matter.

## Signal Path

```text
Digital audio → DAC → amplifier → voice-coil current
             → diaphragm motion → air-pressure waves → sound
```
